## Working Papers

### [A Macro-Finance Model of Credit Spreads](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5313973)
<small><em>with Theofanis Papamichalis, Dimitrios P. Tsomocos, and Nikolaos Romanidis</em></small>

**Abstract:**  
Corporate credit spreads fluctuate with the **risk-bearing capacity of financial intermediaries**. Empirically, primary broker-dealer leverage closely tracks the excess bond premium, indicating that balance-sheet conditions—rather than firm default fundamentals—drive the dominant component of spreads. We build a continuous-time heterogeneous-agent model in which experts finance capital with defaultable debt and face an equity constraint and endogenous default penalties; the model resolves the spread wedge, shows default is non-neutral for investment and volatility, and characterizes optimal policy.

### [Our Deficit, Your Problem: Fiscal Sustainability and Exchange Rates](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5395444)
<small><em>with Tobias Kawalec</em></small>

**Abstract:**  
We develop and estimate an **open-economy present-value framework** for the government budget constraint that embeds discount factors, exchange-rate expectations, and time-varying FX risk premia. Using new market-value data for U.K. and U.S. public debt (1975–2024), we find unexpected changes in the debt ratio split roughly equally between revisions to expected future surpluses and **discount-rate news** (global real yields, expected real exchange rates, and UIP-premium shocks). Surplus innovations materially move bilateral real exchange rates. A tractable two-country model shows fiscal shocks in a financial hegemon propagate internationally via exchange-rate adjustments that feed back into real rates, implying **fiscal contagion** across sovereign balance sheets.

### [The Inequality Multiplier: Market Inelasticity and the Persistence of Wealth Inequality](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5310388)
<small><em>with Aditya Khemka</em></small>

**Abstract:**  
We study how changes in U.S. equity-market macrostructure affect market capitalization, aggregate debt, and wealth inequality. In a general-equilibrium model, **inelastic demand** creates an “inequality multiplier”: (i) an **equity-investment channel**—higher saving by wealthy households amplifies equity booms; and (ii) a **borrowing channel**—higher indebtedness lifts equity prices as intermediaries rebalance. Calibrated to U.S. data, the mechanism makes wealth inequality self-perpetuating and widens the income–wealth gap; it matches trends in equity prices, debt, and concentration.
